About Georgia Income Tax (2026)

Georgia switched from a graduated income tax system (with rates from 1% to 5.75%) to a flat rate structure beginning in 2024. In 2026, the flat rate is 5.49%, down from 5.75% previously. Under Georgia's HB 1437, the rate will continue decreasing by 0.1 percentage points per year, reaching a target of 4.99% by 2029 — subject to revenue triggers. Georgia is one of several Southern states actively reducing its income tax burden to attract businesses and residents.

Key Georgia Tax Facts

  • Flat rate simplifies paycheck math: At 5.49%, every Georgia worker pays the same percentage of income in state tax. Payroll departments no longer need to look up which bracket applies — it's a single rate times taxable income. This was one argument proponents of the flat tax transition made.
  • No local income tax: Georgia has no municipal or county income tax. Atlanta, Savannah, Augusta, and all other Georgia cities do not levy a separate wage tax. This distinguishes Georgia from Ohio and Pennsylvania where local taxes significantly impact take-home pay.
  • Georgia's standard deduction: For 2026, Georgia's standard deduction is $5,400 for single filers — lower than the federal $15,000, meaning more income is subject to Georgia state tax than to federal income tax at the same salary level.

FAQ

Georgia take-home pay questions answered

Georgia's income tax rate for 2026 is a flat 5.49%, applied to all taxable income. Georgia switched from a graduated tax system (six brackets up to 5.75%) to a flat structure starting in 2024 under HB 1437. The rate is scheduled to continue decreasing by approximately 0.1 percentage points per year toward a target of 4.99% by 2029, subject to meeting state revenue thresholds. There is no local income tax in any Georgia city or county — only the state flat rate applies on top of federal obligations.
Georgia's 5.49% flat rate sits above North Carolina (4.5% flat), roughly equal to the middle of South Carolina (0%–6.4% graduated), and well above Florida and Tennessee (both 0% on wages). Among Georgia's direct neighbors, only Alabama (up to 5%) comes close. Georgia's ongoing rate reduction trajectory is a competitive response to Florida's zero-rate and North Carolina's accelerated tax cuts. Workers earning $75,000 in Georgia pay roughly $4,118 per year in state income tax — about $750 more than a comparable North Carolina worker.
Take-home pay (also called net pay) is the amount deposited in your bank account after your employer withholds all taxes and deductions. It equals gross pay minus federal income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), state income tax, and any pre-tax deductions like 401(k) or health insurance premiums. For most US workers, take-home pay is 65–80% of gross pay.
The most effective strategies: (1) Maximize traditional 401(k) contributions — every dollar reduces your federal and state taxable income immediately. (2) Contribute to a Health FSA or HSA through payroll deduction — these reduce both income tax and FICA taxes, saving ~30 cents per dollar. (3) Review your W-4 withholding allowances — if you consistently receive a large tax refund, you are over-withholding and can adjust to keep more each paycheck. Use the pre-tax deductions section in this calculator to model the exact impact.
Gross pay is your total earnings for the pay period before any deductions — your full salary, hourly wages, or other compensation before the government or your employer takes anything out. Net pay (take-home pay) is what you actually receive after federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax, and any voluntary pre-tax deductions are subtracted. For example, a $60,000 annual salary is $2,308 bi-weekly gross — but take-home might be $1,650–$1,900 depending on your state, filing status, and deductions.
