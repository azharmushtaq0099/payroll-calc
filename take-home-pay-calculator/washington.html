About Washington State Taxes (2026)

Washington State has no individual income tax and no tax on wages, salaries, or retirement income. The Washington State Constitution has historically been interpreted to require uniformity in taxation, which has made it legally difficult to impose a graduated income tax. Washington does have unique tax features — a 7% capital gains excise tax (on investment gains over $250,000, not wages), a Business & Occupation (B&O) tax on gross business revenue, and starting in 2022, a Long-Term Care payroll deduction of 0.58% of wages for most workers.

Key Washington State Tax Facts

  • No state income tax on wages — unique in the West: Among Pacific Coast states, only Washington (and Nevada, to the south) imposes no income tax. Oregon has rates up to 9.9%, and California up to 13.3%. A Seattle tech worker at $150,000 keeps roughly $14,000–$19,000 more per year than the same worker in San Francisco, solely due to state income tax.
  • WA Cares Fund (Long-Term Care): Starting in 2023, Washington workers pay 0.58% of wages into the WA Cares Fund, which funds long-term care benefits. On a $75,000 salary, that's $435/year. Workers who purchased qualifying private long-term care insurance before November 2021 and applied for exemption are exempt. This deduction is not reflected in this calculator's state tax field — you can use the pre-tax deduction section as a workaround.
  • Capital gains tax doesn't affect your paycheck: Washington's 7% capital gains excise tax (upheld by the WA Supreme Court in 2023) applies only to net capital gains above $250,000 per year from the sale of stocks, bonds, and similar assets. Wages, salaries, retirement distributions, and real estate gains are all exempt. It has no effect on your biweekly paycheck calculation.

FAQ

Washington State take-home pay questions answered

No — Washington State has no individual income tax on wages, salaries, tips, or most other forms of earned income. Washington's constitution has historically made it difficult to impose a graduated income tax. Seattle and other WA cities also have no local income tax. Workers in Washington pay only federal income tax and FICA (Social Security + Medicare). Note: Washington does have a 7% capital gains excise tax on net investment gains above $250,000 per year, but this does not apply to wages or retirement income and has no effect on your paycheck.
The WA Cares Fund is Washington State's long-term care insurance program, funded by a payroll deduction of 0.58% of gross wages. Starting in 2023, most Washington employees have this amount withheld each paycheck. On a $75,000 salary, that's approximately $435 per year ($16.73 per biweekly paycheck). The program provides up to $36,500 in lifetime long-term care benefits. Workers who purchased qualifying private long-term care insurance before November 1, 2021, and applied for an exemption by a deadline, are permanently exempt. Self-employed workers can opt in voluntarily. This deduction is separate from state income tax and appears as a distinct line item on Washington pay stubs.
Nine states have no individual income tax in 2026: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. Residents of these states pay only federal income tax and FICA — no state withholding at all. Compare that to California (up to 13.3%) or New York (up to 10.9%) and the take-home difference on a $100,000 salary can exceed $8,000 per year.
The most effective strategies: (1) Maximize traditional 401(k) contributions — every dollar reduces your federal and state taxable income immediately. (2) Contribute to a Health FSA or HSA through payroll deduction — these reduce both income tax and FICA taxes, saving ~30 cents per dollar. (3) Review your W-4 withholding allowances — if you consistently receive a large tax refund, you are over-withholding and can adjust to keep more each paycheck. Use the pre-tax deductions section in this calculator to model the exact impact.
Gross pay is your total earnings for the pay period before any deductions — your full salary, hourly wages, or other compensation before the government or your employer takes anything out. Net pay (take-home pay) is what you actually receive after federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax, and any voluntary pre-tax deductions are subtracted. For example, a $60,000 annual salary is $2,308 bi-weekly gross — but take-home might be $1,650–$1,900 depending on your state, filing status, and deductions.
