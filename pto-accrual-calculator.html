How PTO accrual works — and how to calculate it

Paid time off (PTO) accrual is the process by which employees earn vacation, sick, or personal days over time. Employers use three primary models — per pay period, per hour worked, and lump sum — each with different implications for payroll accounting, employee scheduling, and legal compliance under the FLSA and state labor laws.

Per pay period accrual is the most common method for salaried employees. An employee earning 15 days annually on a bi-weekly schedule (26 pay periods) accrues 0.5769 days per period. After the 14th pay period they have accumulated 8.08 days. This method aligns PTO earning directly with payroll processing, making it easy to reflect in any payroll system. For semi-monthly payroll (24 periods), the same 15 days becomes 0.625 days per period.

Per hour worked accrual is standard for hourly and part-time employees. A common benchmark is 0.04 hours of PTO per hour worked, which gives a full-time employee (2,080 hrs/year) about 83.2 hours (10.4 days) annually. Several states now mandate minimum sick leave via hourly accrual — Arizona requires 1 hour per 30 hours worked; Michigan 1 per 35; Illinois 1 per 40. See the Overtime Pay Calculator for additional FLSA hour tracking.

Lump sum PTO deposits the full annual balance on January 1 or the employee's anniversary date. It simplifies recordkeeping but creates a larger liability at year-start. If your employer uses a "use-it-or-lose-it" policy, check your state's rules — California treats accrued PTO as earned wages and prohibits any forfeiture policy. Colorado, Nebraska, and North Dakota have similar wage-parity protections.

Cash value of PTO is calculated as: balance days × (hourly rate × 8 hours/day). For example, 10 days of unused PTO at $25/hr is worth $2,000. When paid out, this amount is treated as supplemental wages subject to 22% federal withholding plus FICA. See our Payroll Tax Calculator or Employer Tax Calculator for exact withholding on payout amounts.

FAQ

PTO accrual questions answered

Divide your total annual PTO days by the number of pay periods per year. For a bi-weekly schedule (26 periods), an employee with 15 days of PTO accrues 0.5769 days per period. After 14 periods they have 8.08 days. For semi-monthly payroll (24 periods) the rate is 0.625 days/period; weekly (52 periods) is 0.2885 days/period. The full formula: Accrual rate = Annual PTO days ÷ Pay periods per year. YTD balance = Accrual rate × Current period number.
Yes — PTO payouts are classified as supplemental wages by the IRS. They are subject to federal income tax withholding at a flat 22% (for amounts under $1 million), plus Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), and applicable state income taxes. The withholding applies whether the payout occurs at termination, via an employer cash-out program, or at year-end rollover. Use our Payroll Tax Calculator to estimate the exact net amount after withholding.
California has the strongest protections: accrued PTO is legally equivalent to earned wages, cannot be forfeited under any company policy, and must be paid at the employee's final rate of pay upon termination. Colorado, Nebraska, North Dakota, and Illinois have similar mandatory payout requirements. By contrast, states like Florida, Texas, Georgia, and New York generally allow "use-it-or-lose-it" policies if clearly written in the employee handbook. Always verify the current rules with your state's department of labor — local city ordinances sometimes impose additional obligations.
According to SHRM's annual benefits survey, the national average for combined PTO (vacation + sick) is 14–17 days per year for employees with 1–5 years of service, rising to 18–22 days for those with 6–10 years. As an hourly accrual rate, this equates to roughly 0.054–0.065 hours of PTO per hour worked for a standard 40-hour week. A common statutory benchmark for paid sick leave is 1 hour per 30 hours worked (≈ 0.0333 hrs/hr). Many technology companies and large employers have adopted unlimited PTO policies — though research consistently shows these often result in employees taking fewer days off than traditional accrual plans.
