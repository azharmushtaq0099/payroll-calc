How to fill out your W-4 in 2026

The W-4 form was redesigned in 2020 — replacing the old allowance system (0, 1, 2…) with actual dollar amounts. The new design is more accurate but less intuitive, which is exactly why this calculator exists. Instead of outputting a generic "withholding amount," our tool tells you exactly what to write on each line of the form.

Why the W-4 changed from allowances to dollar amounts

Under the old system, "claiming 1 allowance" reduced withholding by a fixed amount per allowance ($4,300 in 2019). The problem: that flat reduction didn't match reality for people with varying incomes, multiple jobs, or significant investment income. The new W-4 asks you to enter actual estimated income and deduction amounts — so your employer's withholding calculation more closely mirrors what you'll actually owe at tax time. The 2026 standard deductions are $15,000 (single/MFS), $30,000 (married filing jointly), and $22,500 (head of household).

What each W-4 step does

Step 1 is just your personal info and filing status. Single and Married Filing Separately use the same withholding tables. Head of Household gets a slightly more favorable table. Married Filing Jointly gets the most favorable (lowest withholding) table — which can under-withhold if both spouses work.

Step 2 addresses the multi-job gap. When both spouses work (or you have a second job), each employer withholds as if that job's income is your entire income. But combined income puts you in higher brackets. Checking Step 2(c) triggers the employer to use a higher withholding table. It works best when both jobs have similar pay. For unequal incomes, use Step 4(c) to add a precise per-period dollar amount instead.

Step 3 reduces your withholding for dependent credits. Enter $2,000 per qualifying child under 17, plus $500 per other dependent. If you have two children under 17, enter $4,000. This tells the employer to reduce your annual withholding by that amount (spread across all pay periods).

Steps 4(a), 4(b), and 4(c) are fine-tuning tools. Step 4(a) adds investment or freelance income so it gets withheld for. Step 4(b) reduces withholding if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction. Step 4(c) is a flat extra per-period amount for any remaining gap.

Common W-4 mistakes

Leaving everything blank: This is the equivalent of "claiming 0" — maximum withholding, largest refund, but you're giving the IRS an interest-free loan all year. Some people prefer the forced savings. Most would rather have accurate withholding.

Not updating after life changes: Marriage, divorce, a new baby, a pay raise, a second job, or selling investments that trigger capital gains — all of these change your tax liability. The IRS recommends checking your withholding after any major event. You can submit a new W-4 to your employer at any time, and changes take effect on the next payroll cycle.

When to update your W-4

Update your W-4 when you: get married or divorced, have or adopt a child, start a second job (or your spouse starts working), receive a large bonus or one-time income event, begin receiving investment income, change your deduction strategy (start or stop itemizing), or owe a large tax bill or receive a very large refund. Use our payroll tax calculator to see your per-paycheck withholding after updating, and the take-home pay calculator to see the net effect on your paycheck. FAQ

W-4 questions answered

Use the wizard above for exact amounts, then transfer them to the IRS Form W-4. Step 1: enter name, address, SSN, and filing status. Step 2: check the box if you have multiple jobs or a working spouse. Step 3: enter your dependent credits (children under 17 × $2,000 + other dependents × $500). Step 4(a): other annual income not from jobs. Step 4(b): deductions above the standard deduction. Step 4(c): any extra withholding per pay period. Sign and give the completed form to your employer's payroll or HR department.
The 2020 and later W-4 no longer uses allowances — there is no "0 or 1" to claim. The new form uses actual dollar amounts. Leaving Steps 3 and 4 completely blank is the equivalent of the old "claim 0" (maximum withholding). To optimize withholding — neither over- nor under-withholding — use this calculator to get the specific dollar amounts for each step. The goal is to have your annual withholding match your annual tax liability as closely as possible.
Step 2 addresses the multiple-jobs problem. When you or your household have income from more than one job, each employer withholds based only on that job's income — ignoring the other. Since higher combined income hits higher tax brackets, the default withholding isn't enough. Checking Step 2(c) tells your employer to use a higher withholding table. It works best when both jobs pay roughly the same. For unequal incomes, using Step 4(c) to add a specific extra amount per period is more precise. Our calculator handles both scenarios and tells you which approach to use.
You can submit a new W-4 to your employer at any time — there is no annual limit on how many times you can update it. Download Form W-4 from irs.gov or get it from your HR department. Fill in the new values, sign it, and return it. Changes take effect on the next payroll cycle (some employers may take up to 30 days). If you're behind on withholding mid-year, you can temporarily increase Step 4(c) to catch up, then reduce it next year.
If you don't submit a W-4, your employer must withhold at the single filer rate with no adjustments — the highest possible withholding rate. This guarantees a refund at tax time but reduces every paycheck unnecessarily. If you're starting a new job, you typically must fill out a W-4 as part of onboarding paperwork. If you already have a W-4 on file from a prior year, your employer continues using that — but if your situation has changed (new baby, marriage, pay raise), it's worth updating to avoid a large refund or unexpected tax bill.
