2026 Overtime Pay Rules: What Changed and What to Know

Overtime regulations affecting US employers and employees in 2026 include the ongoing implementation of the DOL's revised salary threshold rules and continued expansion of state-level overtime protections. Here's what employers and hourly workers need to know.

By FreePayrollCalc Editorial • Updated September 2026 • 7 min read

2026 FLSA salary threshold

Under the DOL's final rule, the standard salary level for white-collar overtime exemptions remains at $684 per week ($35,568 annually) as legal challenges to higher thresholds have led to the previous increases being vacated. Employers should watch the DOL's rulemaking calendar for potential updates.

The Highly Compensated Employee (HCE) threshold remains at $107,432 per year, with at least $684/week paid on a salary basis.

How overtime pay is calculated in 2026

The calculation hasn't changed — 1.5× the regular rate of pay for hours over 40 in a workweek:

  • Simple hourly: $20/hr × 1.5 = $30/hr overtime rate. 45 hours = 40 regular ($800) + 5 overtime ($150) = $950
  • With non-discretionary bonus: Regular rate = (base wages + bonus) ÷ total hours. Multiply by 0.5 for overtime premium, then by OT hours.
  • Piece rate workers: Divide total piece rate earnings by total hours worked to get regular rate. Apply 0.5× to regular rate × overtime hours.

State-level overtime updates in 2026

  • California: No change to daily overtime (1.5× after 8hrs, 2× after 12hrs). Minimum wage reached $16.50/hr statewide in 2025, affecting the base overtime rate.
  • Colorado: COMPS Order continues to cover employers with gross revenues over $1M and includes daily overtime protections.
  • Washington, Oregon: Continue to have overtime salary thresholds linked to their minimum wages, currently above the federal $684/week.

Top employer overtime compliance risks

  • Misclassifying non-exempt employees as exempt based on job title rather than actual duties
  • Failing to include non-discretionary bonuses in the regular rate of pay
  • Averaging hours over multiple workweeks
  • Not counting pre- and post-shift activities (donning/doffing, boot up time) as compensable time
  • Off-the-clock work (reading work emails on personal time, etc.)

Frequently Asked Questions

The current FLSA standard salary level for overtime exemptions is $684 per week ($35,568/year). Employees earning below this threshold cannot be classified as exempt from overtime regardless of their job duties, and must receive overtime for hours over 40 in a workweek.
The core FLSA overtime rules remain the same: 1.5× regular rate for hours over 40 per workweek. The DOL's attempt to raise the salary threshold to $1,128/week was vacated by federal courts, returning to $684/week. Monitor the DOL's website for any new rulemaking. Several states have their own higher thresholds — check your specific state's labor department.
