2026 Federal Income Tax Brackets: Complete Guide

The IRS adjusted all seven federal income tax bracket thresholds upward for 2026 to account for inflation, while the standard deduction stays at $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly. Understanding how these brackets actually work — and what your real tax bill looks like — requires knowing the difference between your marginal rate and your effective rate.

How the US Progressive Tax System Works

The United States uses a progressive marginal tax system, which means you do not pay your top bracket rate on your entire income. Instead, each bracket rate applies only to the slice of income that falls within its range. When someone says they're "in the 22% bracket," they mean their highest dollar of income is taxed at 22% — not that they owe 22% of everything they earned.

This distinction matters enormously when you're planning pay raises, retirement contributions, or business income. A single filer with $70,000 of taxable income pays exactly the same tax on their first $12,250 as a filer with $500,000 of taxable income — the higher earner just keeps going up the ladder.

The IRS adjusts bracket thresholds every year using the chained Consumer Price Index (C-CPI-U). This prevents "bracket creep," which is when inflation alone — not actual purchasing power gains — pushes workers into higher tax brackets.

Married Filing Separately (MFS)

Married filing separately uses the same bracket thresholds as single filers for the 10% through 35% brackets. However, the 37% rate kicks in at $387,600 — exactly half of the MFJ threshold — which reflects the IRS anti-stacking rule for joint filers.

MFS Penalty: Married filing separately often results in a higher overall tax bill for couples. Both spouses lose access to several credits (earned income credit, American opportunity credit, student loan interest deduction) and face lower phaseout thresholds for others. MFS is usually beneficial only in specific circumstances such as income-driven student loan repayment.

2026 Standard Deduction Amounts

Before applying bracket rates, most taxpayers subtract the standard deduction from gross income. The standard deduction is the no-documentation alternative to itemizing deductions such as mortgage interest, state taxes, and charitable contributions. About 90% of US households take the standard deduction.

Marginal Rate vs. Effective Rate: The Critical Distinction

Your marginal rate is the rate on your last dollar of income. Your effective rate is total tax divided by total income. These are always different numbers for any taxpayer who doesn't live entirely in the 10% bracket.

Why does this matter? When people say "getting a raise will push me into a higher bracket," they're confusing marginal and effective rates. Moving into the 22% bracket only means the dollars above the bracket threshold are taxed at 22% — not your entire paycheck. A $1,000 raise that crosses a bracket boundary does not make your previous income more expensive.

Step-by-Step: Calculating Tax for an $85,000 Single Filer

Let's walk through a complete federal income tax calculation for a single filer with $85,000 in wages, no itemized deductions, and a standard W-4.

Step 1: Calculate Adjusted Gross Income (AGI)

For this example, we'll assume no above-the-line deductions (no student loan interest, no traditional IRA contribution, etc.), so AGI equals gross income: $85,000.

Step 2: Subtract the Standard Deduction

Taxable income = $85,000 − $15,000 standard deduction = $70,000

Step 3: Apply the Brackets

10% on $12,250 (the full first bracket) = $1,225.00
12% on $37,500 ($12,251 to $49,750) = $4,500.00
22% on $20,250 ($49,751 to $70,000) = $4,455.00

Total federal income tax: $10,180.00

Step 4: Determine Your Rates

  • Marginal rate: 22% (the bracket containing the last dollar of income)
  • Effective rate: $10,180 ÷ $85,000 = 11.98%

Notice the effective rate is almost half the marginal rate. This is the power of the progressive bracket system — even though this filer is technically "in the 22% bracket," they only pay 22% on $20,250 of their $70,000 taxable income.

State Income Tax: One More Layer

Federal brackets are only part of the picture. Forty-one states plus Washington D.C. levy a state income tax on top of your federal obligation. Nine states — Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming — have no state income tax on wages.

State rates vary widely. A single filer earning $85,000 in California pays 9.3% state income tax on a substantial portion of income (California's top rate of 13.3% kicks in above $1 million). The same worker in Texas owes $0 in state income tax. When estimating take-home pay, always factor in your specific state's rates using a state-specific calculator.

SALT deduction cap: The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act capped the State and Local Tax (SALT) deduction at $10,000 per year for taxpayers who itemize. This cap is set to expire December 31, 2025 — Congress is debating whether to extend, modify, or eliminate it as of late 2026.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a marginal and effective tax rate?

Your marginal rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income — the bracket you are in. Your effective rate is total tax paid divided by total gross income. Because the US uses a progressive system, only income above each bracket threshold is taxed at the higher rate. A single filer with $85,000 of gross income has a 22% marginal rate but pays only about 11.98% of their total income in federal tax.

What are the 2026 federal income tax brackets for married filing jointly?

For married filing jointly in 2026: 10% on income up to $24,500; 12% on $24,501–$99,500; 22% on $99,501–$212,300; 24% on $212,301–$405,200; 32% on $405,201–$515,300; 35% on $515,301–$775,200; and 37% on income over $775,200. These thresholds apply to taxable income after subtracting the $30,000 standard deduction.

What is the 2026 standard deduction?

The 2026 standard deduction is $15,000 for single filers and married filing separately, $30,000 for married filing jointly, and $22,500 for head of household. Taxpayers 65 or older and those who are legally blind receive an additional amount — $1,600 per qualifying status for single or HoH filers, $1,350 for married filers.

Do I pay 22% on all my income if I am in the 22% bracket?

No. The US tax system is marginal — you pay each rate only on the slice of income within that bracket. A single filer with $70,000 of taxable income pays 10% on the first $12,250, 12% on the next $37,500, and only 22% on the remaining $20,250. Moving into a higher bracket never makes previously earned income more expensive.

How much did the 2026 federal tax brackets change from 2025?

All bracket thresholds rose approximately 2.7% for 2026 to account for inflation measured by the chained CPI-U. The standard deduction held steady at $15,000 (single) and $30,000 (MFJ). The seven bracket rates — 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37% — did not change; only the income thresholds moved up.
