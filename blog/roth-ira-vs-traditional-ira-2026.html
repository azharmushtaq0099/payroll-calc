Roth IRA vs. Traditional IRA: Which Is Better for You in 2026?

The core difference between a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA is when you pay tax: Roth contributions are made with after-tax dollars and grow tax-free, while Traditional contributions may be deductible now but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. The right choice depends entirely on whether your tax rate is higher today or in retirement — and the honest answer is that most people should consider both.

2026 IRA Contribution Limits

The contribution limits for both IRA types are identical and set by the IRS each year. For 2026:

AgeAnnual Contribution LimitNotes
Under 50$7,000Combined limit across all IRAs
50 and older$8,000Includes $1,000 catch-up contribution

This $7,000 / $8,000 limit is a combined limit across all your IRA accounts. If you have both a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA, your total contributions to both cannot exceed $7,000 in 2026 (or $8,000 if 50+). You can split the amount however you like — $3,500 each, $7,000 to one and $0 to the other, etc.

You must have earned income at least equal to your IRA contribution. Wages, salaries, self-employment income, and alimony under certain agreements count. Investment income, Social Security, and pensions do not.

When Roth IRA Wins

The Roth IRA is the more powerful account when your tax rate in retirement will be higher than your tax rate today. In that scenario, paying tax now at the lower rate beats paying tax later at the higher rate on a much larger balance.

Choose Roth IRA When…

  • You're early in your career and in a low tax bracket (10% or 12%)
  • You expect significant income growth or bracket expansion
  • You want flexibility — Roth contributions can be withdrawn anytime
  • You want to avoid RMDs and leave tax-free money to heirs
  • You have a long time horizon for tax-free compounding
  • Current tax rates are historically low and you expect them to rise

Choose Traditional IRA When…

  • You're at peak earnings and in the 24%–35% bracket
  • You expect lower income in retirement (especially early retirees)
  • You need the upfront deduction to reduce taxable income now
  • You are close to a tax bracket threshold and a deduction keeps you below it
  • Your state has high income taxes now but you'll retire in a no-tax state

2026 Income Phase-Out Ranges

Roth IRA Contribution Phase-Out

Your ability to contribute directly to a Roth IRA is reduced — and eventually eliminated — above certain modified AGI (MAGI) thresholds:

Filing StatusPhase-Out BeginsPhase-Out EndsAbove Limit
Single / Head of Household$150,000$165,000No direct contribution allowed
Married Filing Jointly$236,000$246,000No direct contribution allowed
Married Filing Separately$0$10,000No direct contribution allowed

Within the phase-out range, your allowable contribution is reduced proportionally. For example, a single filer with $157,500 MAGI is halfway through the phase-out range and can contribute $3,500 to a Roth IRA (50% of the $7,000 limit).

Traditional IRA Deductibility Phase-Out

Anyone with earned income can contribute to a Traditional IRA, but the deductibility of that contribution depends on whether you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace retirement plan:

SituationPhase-Out Range (2026)
Single / HoH — covered by workplace plan$79,000 – $89,000 MAGI
MFJ — spouse covered by workplace plan$126,000 – $136,000 MAGI
MFJ — you are NOT covered, but spouse IS$236,000 – $246,000 MAGI
Neither spouse covered by workplace planNo phase-out — fully deductible

Above the phase-out range, Traditional IRA contributions are nondeductible. You can still contribute; the money grows tax-deferred, and you track the cost basis on Form 8606 to avoid double-taxing it at withdrawal.

The Tax Breakeven Analysis: When Does Roth Beat Traditional?

The fundamental math of the Roth vs. Traditional decision comes down to one question: will you pay more tax now (Roth) or more tax later (Traditional)?

Assume you contribute $7,000 to an IRA, it grows to $35,000 over 20 years (5x growth), and you withdraw everything in retirement. Here's how the two accounts compare at different tax rate scenarios:

Contribution RateWithdrawal RateRoth After-Tax ValueTraditional After-Tax ValueWinner
12%22%$35,000$27,300Roth wins
22%22%$35,000$35,000Tie (use Roth for flexibility)
24%12%$35,000$42,000*Traditional wins
32%22%$35,000$38,500*Traditional wins

*Traditional value assumes tax savings on contribution were invested and also grew. The comparison includes reinvesting the deduction at the same return rate.

The breakeven point is when your contribution rate equals your withdrawal rate. If you're in the 22% bracket now and expect to be in the 22% bracket in retirement, both accounts produce identical after-tax wealth — but Roth gives you more flexibility (no RMDs, accessible contributions without penalty). When in doubt at identical rates, most financial planners prefer Roth for younger savers.

The RMD Advantage of the Roth IRA

One often-overlooked Roth advantage is the complete absence of Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) during the account owner's lifetime. Traditional IRA owners must begin withdrawing a minimum amount each year starting at age 73, calculated using IRS life expectancy tables. These mandatory withdrawals are taxable and can push retirees into higher brackets, trigger Medicare IRMAA surcharges, and increase the taxability of Social Security benefits.

A large Roth IRA balance lets you control the timing of every withdrawal. If you have a low-income year in retirement, you can withdraw nothing from the Roth and let it compound further. If you have a high-income year, you can still draw on the Roth without adding to your taxable income. This flexibility has real monetary value, particularly for retirees managing multi-account income strategies.

The Backdoor Roth IRA: No Income Limit

High-income earners above the Roth IRA phase-out limits are not permanently locked out. The backdoor Roth IRA is a legal, IRS-acknowledged strategy that works in two steps:

  1. Make a nondeductible contribution to a Traditional IRA. There is no income limit on nondeductible Traditional IRA contributions — anyone with earned income can do this.
  2. Convert the Traditional IRA to a Roth IRA. IRA conversions have no income limit. You'll owe income tax on any pre-tax money converted (earnings between step 1 and step 2), but if you convert immediately after contributing, the taxable amount is typically close to zero.

The key risk is the pro-rata rule: if you have other pre-tax Traditional IRA balances (not in a 401k — those don't count), the IRS treats all your IRAs as one pool. Your conversion will be partially taxable based on the ratio of pre-tax to after-tax money across all your IRAs. If you have $63,000 in a pre-tax rollover IRA and contribute $7,000 nondeductible, only 10% of your conversion ($700) will be tax-free.

SECURE 2.0 and IRA changes: The SECURE 2.0 Act raised the RMD age from 72 to 73 (effective 2023) and to 75 for those turning 74 after December 31, 2032. It also eliminated RMDs for Roth accounts in workplace plans (Roth 401k) starting in 2024, making Roth 401k balances behave more like Roth IRAs.

Inherited IRA Rules: Another Reason to Prefer Roth

When a non-spouse inherits a Traditional IRA, the SECURE Act (2019) eliminated the "stretch IRA" strategy. Inherited Traditional IRAs must now be fully withdrawn within 10 years, and all withdrawals are taxable. For a beneficiary at peak earnings, this can mean a substantial tax hit.

An inherited Roth IRA also requires full withdrawal within 10 years for non-spouse beneficiaries — but those withdrawals are completely tax-free. If leaving wealth to the next generation is part of your plan, the Roth IRA's tax-free inheritance feature is a significant advantage.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I contribute to both a Roth IRA and a Traditional IRA in 2026?

Yes. You can split contributions between both IRA types in the same year, but your total across all IRA accounts cannot exceed $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50 or older). For example, $4,000 to Roth and $3,000 to Traditional is fine; $7,000 to each is not allowed.

What is the 2026 Roth IRA income limit?

Direct Roth IRA contributions phase out between $150,000 and $165,000 MAGI for single filers, and between $236,000 and $246,000 for married filing jointly. Above the upper threshold, you cannot contribute directly to a Roth IRA, but you can use the backdoor Roth IRA strategy instead.

Is a Traditional IRA deductible if I have a 401(k) at work?

It depends on your MAGI. For 2026, if you are covered by a workplace retirement plan, the deduction phases out between $79,000 and $89,000 (single) or $126,000 and $136,000 (MFJ). Above those limits, contributions are nondeductible but still grow tax-deferred.

What is a backdoor Roth IRA?

A backdoor Roth is a two-step strategy for high-income earners: (1) make a nondeductible Traditional IRA contribution — no income limit applies — then (2) immediately convert it to a Roth IRA. The conversion is taxable only on any earnings between contribution and conversion. Watch out for the pro-rata rule if you have other pre-tax IRA balances.

Which is better — a Roth or Traditional IRA?

If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement than you are today, Roth wins — you pay tax now at the lower rate and never pay tax on growth. If you're at peak earnings and expect a lower bracket in retirement, Traditional wins because the deduction today offsets more tax than the future tax bill. Many advisors recommend using both to diversify tax exposure across retirement income.
