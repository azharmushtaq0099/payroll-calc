Self-Employment Tax in 2026: Complete Guide for Freelancers and Sole Proprietors

If you earn $400 or more in self-employment income in a year, you must pay self-employment (SE) tax. Unlike employees who split FICA 50/50 with their employer, self-employed individuals pay both sides — a combined 15.3% on net self-employment earnings.

By FreePayrollCalc Editorial • Updated September 2026 • 9 min read

What is self-employment tax?

Self-employment tax is the self-employed person's equivalent of FICA tax. It consists of:

  • Social Security tax: 12.4% on net self-employment income up to $176,100 (2026 wage base)
  • Medicare tax: 2.9% on all net self-employment income
  • Additional Medicare: 0.9% on net SE income above $200,000 (single) / $250,000 (MFJ)
  • Total base rate: 15.3% (12.4% + 2.9%)

How to calculate SE tax step by step

  1. Calculate net self-employment income: Total business revenue − allowable business deductions
  2. Multiply by 92.35%: Net SE income × 0.9235 = SE tax base. (This accounts for the employer-side deduction: you only pay SE tax on 92.35% of earnings, just as employers pay FICA on wages but wages are the full amount.)
  3. Apply 15.3% to the SE tax base (up to $176,100; 2.9% only above that)
  4. Deduct half of SE tax from gross income: You get to deduct 50% of SE tax as an above-the-line income tax deduction on Schedule 1, reducing your federal income tax bill
Example: Freelancer with $80,000 net SE income (2026)
SE tax base = $80,000 × 0.9235 = $73,880
SE tax = $73,880 × 15.3% = $11,304
Deduction for half SE tax = $11,304 ÷ 2 = $5,652
Federal taxable income reduced by $5,652

Quarterly estimated tax payments

Self-employed individuals must make quarterly estimated tax payments (Form 1040-ES) if they expect to owe $1,000 or more. 2026 due dates:

  • Q1 (Jan 1 – Mar 31): Due April 15, 2026
  • Q2 (Apr 1 – May 31): Due June 16, 2026
  • Q3 (Jun 1 – Aug 31): Due September 15, 2026
  • Q4 (Sep 1 – Dec 31): Due January 15, 2027

Legal ways to reduce self-employment taxes

  • S-Corp election: At roughly $40,000+ in net SE income, electing S-Corp status and paying yourself a reasonable salary (subject to FICA) while taking distributions above salary can save thousands in SE tax. The distributions are not subject to SE tax.
  • SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k): Contributing to a SEP-IRA (up to 25% of net SE income, max $69,000 in 2026) or Solo 401(k) reduces your taxable income dollar-for-dollar and also reduces SE tax in some structures.
  • Maximize business deductions: Home office, vehicle (actual expenses or standard mileage at 67¢/mile in 2025), equipment (Section 179), professional development, health insurance premium deduction.
