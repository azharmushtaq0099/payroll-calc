401(k) Contribution Limits 2026: Rules, Strategies & Maximizing Your Match

For 2026, you can defer up to $23,500 of your own paycheck into a 401(k), with the total bucket — including your employer's contributions — capped at $70,000. If you are between ages 60 and 63, the SECURE 2.0 Act's "super catch-up" provision lets you contribute up to $34,750 from your own salary — the largest individual 401(k) deferral limit ever.

These limits apply to traditional 401(k), Roth 401(k), and SIMPLE 401(k) plans offered by employers, as well as 403(b) plans for non-profit and school employees and 457(b) plans for government employees. The $23,500 is an aggregate limit across all such plans — it doesn't multiply if you have multiple jobs with 401(k) plans.

What Counts Toward the $23,500 Limit

The $23,500 employee deferral limit includes:

  • Pre-tax (traditional) 401(k) contributions — reduce current taxable income
  • Roth 401(k) contributions — no current deduction, but tax-free withdrawals in retirement
  • Any combination of the two, as long as the total doesn't exceed $23,500

The $23,500 limit does not include:

  • Employer matching contributions
  • Employer profit-sharing contributions
  • After-tax non-Roth contributions (these count toward the $70,000 total limit but not the $23,500 limit)
Multiple jobs with 401(k) plans: If you work two jobs simultaneously and both offer 401(k) plans, your combined elective deferrals to all plans cannot exceed $23,500. Excess deferrals (contributions above the limit) must be returned by April 15 of the following year, or they get taxed twice. Monitor your total carefully if you hold multiple W-2 positions.

New for 2026: SECURE 2.0 Super Catch-Up for Ages 60–63

The SECURE 2.0 Act, signed in December 2022, created a higher catch-up contribution limit for employees in the four years spanning ages 60, 61, 62, and 63. For 2026, this super catch-up is $11,250 — compared to the regular $7,500 catch-up for ages 50 and over.

This means an employee who turns 60 in 2026 can defer up to:

$23,500 (base limit) + $11,250 (super catch-up) = $34,750 from paycheck

The super catch-up window is specifically ages 60–63. When you turn 64, you drop back to the regular $7,500 catch-up (total $31,000). The provision is designed to help people in the final sprint before traditional retirement age accelerate tax-advantaged savings.

Note: As of 2026, plan sponsors are required to offer catch-up contributions as Roth (after-tax) for employees earning over $145,000 in the prior year. Check with your plan administrator to confirm your plan's implementation of this provision.

How Employer Matching Works

An employer match is essentially a compensation supplement — free money added to your retirement account based on how much you contribute. Matches vary widely between employers but follow common formulas:

The Most Common Match Structure: 50% of First 6%

An employer who matches "50% of the first 6%" means they contribute $0.50 for every dollar you put in, up to 6% of your salary. To receive the full match, you must contribute at least 6% of your salary yourself.

If you contribute only 3%, the employer adds $1,125 — you've left $1,125 of free money on the table. This is why the most universal retirement advice is: always contribute enough to get the full employer match.

How to Calculate Your Contribution Percentage to Get the Full Match

Your 401(k) election is set as a percentage of each paycheck, not a dollar amount. To find the right percentage:

  1. Identify your employer's match threshold (e.g., "up to 6% of salary")
  2. Elect at least that percentage on your 401(k) enrollment form (set to 6% in this example)
  3. Verify with payroll: Annual salary × 6% should equal your projected year contributions. Divide by pay periods to check each paycheck deduction.

Example: $80,000 salary × 6% = $4,800/year. Bi-weekly = $4,800 ÷ 26 = $184.62 per paycheck. If your pay stub shows less than $184.62 under "401(k)," you're not getting the full match.

Traditional vs. Roth 401(k): Which to Choose

Many employers now offer both traditional and Roth 401(k) options within the same plan. The contribution limit ($23,500) applies to the combined total regardless of which type you choose.

The Mega Backdoor Roth 401(k)

If your employer's plan allows it, the mega backdoor Roth strategy lets you contribute significantly more than the $23,500 employee deferral limit to a Roth account. Here's how it works:

  1. Contribute your full $23,500 in regular deferrals (traditional or Roth 401k)
  2. Add after-tax non-Roth contributions to your 401(k). These are additional contributions beyond the $23,500 limit but within the $70,000 total additions cap. The maximum after-tax contribution is the $70,000 total limit minus your deferrals minus employer contributions: e.g., $70,000 − $23,500 − $8,000 employer match = $38,500 of after-tax room.
  3. Convert the after-tax contributions to Roth (in-plan Roth rollover or, if allowed, roll to a Roth IRA). Growth is tax-free from the conversion date forward.

Not all 401(k) plans allow after-tax contributions or in-plan Roth conversions. Check your Summary Plan Description (SPD) or ask your HR team. If available, this is one of the most powerful tax-advantaged savings tools available to high-income earners who have already maxed out their standard 401(k) and IRA contributions.

What Happens If You Over-Contribute

Excess deferrals — contributions above the $23,500 limit — trigger the IRS correction process:

  • Deadline to correct: April 15 of the year after the excess contribution. Your plan administrator must distribute the excess plus allocable earnings by that date.
  • Tax treatment: The excess is included in your income for the year of contribution (it was never excluded from W-2 wages properly). Earnings on the excess are taxable in the year of distribution. If the excess is not corrected timely, you're taxed in both the contribution year AND the distribution year — double taxation.
  • When it happens: Most often occurs when employees change jobs mid-year and contribute to two 401(k) plans, unaware that the $23,500 limit applies in aggregate. Your new employer has no way of knowing your prior year contributions.

The cleanest way to avoid this: track your year-to-date 401(k) contributions in your benefits portal or on your pay stubs and stop contributions when you approach the limit. Many payroll systems will auto-stop at the legal limit, but this doesn't work if you have multiple employers contributing to different plans.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 401(k) contribution limit for 2026?

The 2026 employee deferral limit is $23,500. Employees age 50 and older can add a $7,500 catch-up contribution for a total of $31,000. Those aged exactly 60–63 get the SECURE 2.0 super catch-up of $11,250 instead, for a total of $34,750. Including employer contributions, the total annual additions limit is $70,000 (or $77,500 / $81,250 with catch-up provisions).

Does my employer's match count toward the $23,500 limit?

No. The $23,500 employee deferral limit applies only to contributions from your own paycheck. Your employer's matching contributions are separate and count toward the higher $70,000 total additions limit. Contribute $23,500 and receive an $8,000 match — your total is $31,500, well under the $70,000 cap.

What is the SECURE 2.0 super catch-up contribution?

SECURE 2.0 created a higher catch-up limit specifically for employees aged 60, 61, 62, and 63. For 2026, the super catch-up is $11,250 (vs. $7,500 for age 50+), bringing total employee deferrals to $34,750. When you turn 64, you revert to the regular $7,500 catch-up. The window is precisely ages 60 through 63 — not a day younger or older.

What happens if I contribute too much to my 401(k)?

Excess deferrals must be returned by April 15 of the following year to avoid double taxation. Corrective distributions include allocable earnings and are taxable in the year distributed. This most often happens when changing jobs mid-year. Track your YTD contributions across all plans throughout the year to avoid it.

Should I contribute to a traditional or Roth 401(k)?

Traditional 401(k) reduces taxable income now and is best when you're in a high bracket today. Roth 401(k) builds tax-free wealth and is best when you're in a lower bracket today or expect higher income in retirement. Many planners recommend splitting between both for tax diversification — hedge against future rate changes by having accounts taxed under different regimes.
